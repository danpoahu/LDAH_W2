LDA Hawaiʻi invited Native Hawaiian families to share how special-education information could be made clearer and more culturally responsive. These are the aggregated, anonymous results. With 8 responses, this is an early signal that points us in a clear direction rather than a statistical sample.

What families told us

The need for culturally responsive, plain-language materials is nearly universal. 6 of 6 respondents rate it important or very important, and 6 of 7 say they would feel more comfortable in IEP meetings if materials were translated or explained more simply.

Understanding the IEP process is a real gap. Only 0 of 8 feel they fully understand the IEP process end to end, and 5 of 7 have avoided asking questions in a meeting because the information felt too confusing.

Hawaiian language and culture matter at home. English is spoken in nearly every home, and 5 responses also name ʻOlelo Hawaiʻi or Hawaiian Pidgin. 6 of 7 say weaving in Native Hawaiian cultural values would help their family feel more connected.

Reach. Responses came from Oʻahu (7), Hawaiʻi Island (1).

The takeaway

Even at this early scale, families are sending one clear message: special-education information is hard to navigate, and plain-language, culturally responsive materials — with one-on-one and printed support — would make a real difference. The strongest need is around understanding assessment results, eligibility, and services, and most respondents want to stay engaged through future trainings.
